# Critical Analysis — OLS Confidence Interval Simulation

## Main findings

The 2,000-replicate simulation per setting shows that the OLS slope estimates are close to the true value of 1 across all conditions; mean bias was small (absolute bias at most 0.009 in these runs). Because both methods use the **same coefficient estimator**, their bias and RMSE are identical within each scenario. What differs is the estimated uncertainty and confidence-interval coverage.

For independent normal errors, conventional OLS coverage was 94.75%, 93.70%, and 94.80% at sample sizes 30, 100, and 500. HC3 yielded 93.60%, 93.80%, and 94.95%, respectively. For standardized heavy-tailed errors, conventional coverage was 94.90%, 95.00%, and 94.75%; HC3 coverage was 95.20%, 95.30%, and 94.60%. These are generally compatible with the nominal 95% target given Monte Carlo variability, though the normal n=100 realization is somewhat low.

Heteroskedasticity produced the clearest difference. Conventional coverage was only **81.25%, 80.15%, and 80.90%** at n=30, 100, and 500, whereas HC3 achieved **92.75%, 93.80%, and 95.05%**. At n=500, mean conventional SE was 0.0447 but the empirical sampling SD of the slope was 0.0670; HC3 mean SE was 0.0681. This explains why conventional intervals remain too narrow even in large samples. HC3 intervals are wider under heteroskedasticity, but their coverage is much closer to the requested level. Both methods' RMSE decreases markedly with n.

## Design decisions, fairness, and limitations

The design crosses three sample sizes with three error mechanisms to contrast favorable assumptions, heavy tails, and nonconstant variance. Normal homoskedastic errors offer a theoretical sanity check. The marginal error variance is standardized across mechanisms, avoiding a trivial scale-driven advantage. Both inference procedures evaluate **exactly the same simulated dataset** in every replicate; neither receives different draws or more information. The baseline conventional method uses an exact t critical value under the classical model; HC3 uses the familiar asymptotic normal critical value. This is a reasonable practice-oriented comparison, but it gives the conventional procedure a small-sample advantage under its ideal assumptions, while HC3 also has small-sample approximation error.

The design assumes a correctly specified linear conditional mean, independent observations, and a single continuous predictor. It does not cover omitted-variable bias, endogeneity, nonlinear mean functions, dependent data, extreme leverage contamination, or errors with infinite variance. HC3 corrects standard errors, **not** biased coefficient estimates or sensitivity to outliers. Results for the specified heteroskedastic form should not be generalized to every possible form of heteroskedasticity. With 2,000 independent replications, a coverage estimate near 95% has Monte Carlo SE about 0.49 percentage points, so a difference of about 1 percentage point could be sampling variation. One unexpected observation is that n=500 does not correct conventional undercoverage under heteroskedasticity; increasing n cannot repair a systematically inconsistent variance formula.

## Implementation, confidence, and next steps

The biggest implementation challenge was ensuring a fair comparison and correct robust covariance calculation. In particular, HC3 requires leverage-adjusted squared residuals rather than conventional RSS-based variance. Five automated tests cover the DGP, slope recovery, deterministic reproducibility, valid summary metrics and paired estimates, and invalid inputs. Raw results are saved before summary and visualization; the fixed random seed, parameter dictionary, test suite, and Makefile support independent reruns. These checks increase confidence but cannot exclude a shared modeling or coding mistake, so comparison with a second implementation (such as statsmodels HC3) would be valuable.

If extending the study, I would examine more sample sizes, stronger heteroskedasticity, non-normal conditional errors, clustered observations, and small-sample HC3 variants or bootstrap intervals. The practical lesson is that a point estimate can remain accurate while its nominal confidence interval is misleading when the variance model is wrong; robust standard errors can substantially improve inference in such cases.
